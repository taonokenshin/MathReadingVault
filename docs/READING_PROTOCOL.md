# A compact protocol for sustained mathematical reading

The learner supplies the overall aim and standards. The agent designs the route and individual sessions, and revises them when the mathematics demands it. Start with one coherent book or topic; comparison books support named interfaces rather than merging unrelated subjects.

## A session

- Read a small current checkpoint and the latest relevant completed record. Reuse reliable earlier work and respect source/book gates.
- Choose one natural proof chain, a substantive question, or a few short related items. Prepare one to three adjacent mathematical candidates. Initial item count is a starting plan, not a throughput quota or an automatic stopping point.
- Spend the core on mathematical understanding: unpack hypotheses, derive the decisive step, attempt an exercise, test an edge case or repair a specific gap. In a verification phase, reuse reliable existing proofs rather than redoing them indiscriminately.
- Consult a bounded original-source window when a statement, formula, page boundary or dependency actually needs comparison. Keep source-stated and reader-supplied arguments distinct.
- When the initial goal is complete and useful budget remains, continue an adjacent relevant question. When difficulty grows, reduce the planned count and preserve the unresolved branch honestly.
- Near the session boundary, stop expanding. Save what changed, what was reused, a counterexample or failed route, the exact outstanding step, and the next source/input to open.

The original main-lane pattern targets about 50 minutes of direct mathematics, at least roughly 75% of effective attention, within a roughly 55–60 minute window. It uses fixed batches of twelve qualified occurrences followed by self-pause and manual reactivation. These are local policies that a new learner should adapt, not universal requirements for mathematical learning or a measured learning outcome.

## Status vocabulary

A textbook statement, a cited theorem, a reader argument, a finite experiment, an attempted proof, and a source caveat are different forms of evidence. closed-pass1 and closed-reviewed are historical project labels with explicit scopes; they are not formal proof-assistant certificates. An internal open-hard label does not mean an unsolved problem in the literature. A reader's suspected source error is not an official erratum.

The receiving learner may use the existing material without replaying the old proof-audit campaign. A research supplement is optional. Historical commands and continuations in exported notes are evidence of the old project, never authority to execute them on a new machine.
